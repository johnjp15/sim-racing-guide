# 04: Displays, VR, PC, Console, Audio, Button Boxes, Accessories

Research date: 2026-10-09. US market, USD. All prices and compatibility claims were checked 2026-10 against the linked source unless a different snapshot date is noted. "Shaky" = single source, dated, or sources disagree. Independent of the old `research/` folder.

## Key takeaways

1. 2026 is an expensive year for anything with memory in it: a DRAM shortage pushed up GPUs, RAM, consoles and VR headsets. Budget numbers from 2024 or 2025 guides are wrong now ([Tom's Hardware RAM](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/ddr5/32gb-of-ddr5-now-costs-usd375-minimum-ai-shortage-continues-to-squeeze-pc-building), [HUB via StartupHub](https://www.startuphub.ai/hardware/gpu-prices-rose-8-in-september-and-no-drop-is-near)).
2. Best first display for most beginners: one 34" 1440p ultrawide (about $300 to $450 for VA, $650 to $800 for QD-OLED). No bezels, works in every game, modest GPU load.
3. Correct FOV matters more than screen size. Put the screen close (right behind the wheelbase), measure eye distance, use a calculator. A small screen at 60 cm beats a big one at 150 cm.
4. Triples give the best flat-panel awareness but cost 3x pixels, need a rigid stand, and only work properly in sims with per-screen projection (iRacing, AC, ACC, AC EVO, LMU, AMS2, rF2, RaceRoom). F1 25, EA WRC and Forza only stretch.
5. VR is the cheapest route to full immersion in headset terms (Quest 3S $350, Quest 3 $600) but needs a strong GPU, stable 72 to 90 fps, and tolerance for heat and nausea. Try before committing.
6. Monitors are the one category that got cheaper: 27" 1440p 240 Hz OLEDs have hit about $300 on sale, and good 27" 1440p LCDs sit around $230.
7. Consoles are no longer the cheap on-ramp they were: PS5 $650, PS5 Pro $900, Xbox Series X $800, Series S $500. If you already own one, use it. If buying new for sim racing, a PC is now close in price and far more capable.
8. PC builds as of 2026-10: about $1,150 gets a real 1080p/1440p sim PC, about $1,850 to $2,200 handles ultrawide/entry triples/VR, about $3,200 handles triple 1440p and high-res VR ([PCPartPicker guides](https://pcpartpicker.com/guide/)). Prebuilts are often cheaper than DIY right now.
9. Audio: any decent wired headphones beat TV speakers. Add the free Crew Chief spotter before spending on hardware.
10. Free software does most of what paid hardware does: SimHub on an old phone is a dash display, Crew Chief is a spotter and engineer, a keyboard is a button box. Buy hardware only after you hit a real limit.

Pros and cons:

- **Single 27"/32"**
  - Pros: cheapest, lowest GPU load, works with every game and console, easy to mount on a wheel stand.
  - Cons: narrow view (no side awareness), need a virtual mirror/radar app, tempting to set an unrealistically wide FOV.
- **34" ultrawide**
  - Pros: noticeably more peripheral view, no bezels, one cable, GPU load only about 35% above 1440p, great for non-racing use. RTINGS says 34"+ ultrawides are advantageous for sim racing ([RTINGS buying guide](https://www.rtings.com/monitor/learn/what-to-look-for-gaming-monitor)).
  - Cons: still cannot see a car alongside; consoles do not output 21:9 (PS5/Xbox render 16:9 with black bars, shaky: verify per console).
- **49" super-ultrawide**
  - Pros: about 2x 27" 1440p in one panel, no bezels or alignment, simpler than triples.
  - Cons: a 49" still shows less true side vision than angled triples ([simracingsetup triples vs ultrawide](https://simracingsetup.com/?p=13875390)); edges are distorted in sims without curved-screen projection; RTINGS notes the VA G9 has narrow viewing angles ([RTINGS Odyssey G9](https://www.rtings.com/monitor/reviews/samsung/odyssey-g9)).
- **Triples**
  - Pros: 140 to 170 deg view, you can turn your head to check a car alongside, correct 1:1 scale, no headset on your face.
  - Cons: 3x pixels, three matching monitors, bezels, a rigid stand, careful measurement, 1.6 to 2 m of width, poor support in arcade-leaning titles (see section 3).
- **Big TV**
  - Pros: you may already own it, best choice for console, OLED TVs have superb motion and contrast. LG C6 supports 4K 120 Hz for consoles and up to 165 Hz on PC with VRR ([Tom's Guide C6 pricing](https://www.tomsguide.com/tvs/oled-tvs/the-lg-c6-and-g6-oled-tvs-are-coming-this-month-here-are-the-prices-for-every-screen-size)).
  - Cons: usually sits too far away so real FOV is tiny, cheap TVs have high input lag unless Game Mode is on, low pixel density up close at 42"+.
- **VR**
  - Pros: true depth and scale, natural head look into apexes, takes no floor space, cheapest path to "surround".
  - Cons: heat, pressure on face, cannot see your button box or keyboard, motion sickness for some, heavy GPU demand, fiddly software, isolating for spectators.

## 2. Display fundamentals

### Size, distance, FOV

- FOV = how much of the virtual world is squeezed onto your screen. "Correct" FOV makes objects appear life-size from your seat, so speed, braking distance and corner radius look right.
- Too-wide FOV (the default in most games): everything looks far and slow, you brake late and miss apexes. Too-narrow: tunnel vision, but distances are truthful.
- Correct FOV depends on only two things: screen width and eye-to-screen distance. Closer or bigger screen = wider correct FOV.
- Practical rules:
  - Mount the screen as close as possible, ideally directly behind (or above) the wheelbase. Coach Dave suggests 65 to 80 cm eye distance ([Coach Dave](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/best-sim-racing-monitors/)).
  - Screen centre at or slightly below eye level so the horizon sits at natural eye height ([SimXPro](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/curved-vs-flat-monitors-for-triple-setups-what-actually-works-in-sim-racing)).
  - Fix seat and wheel position first, then place screens, then calculate FOV ([SimXPro](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/curved-vs-flat-monitors-for-triple-setups-what-actually-works-in-sim-racing)).
  - Use a calculator. The common free one takes aspect ratio, size, distance, single/triple/curved, curve radius and bezel width ([dinex86 FOV Calculator](https://dinex86.github.io/FOV-Calculator/)).
- Geometric horizontal FOV for a flat screen (my calculation, not a quoted source):

| Screen | 60 cm | 70 cm | 80 cm | 100 cm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 27" 16:9 (59.8 cm wide) | 53 deg | 46 deg | 41 deg | 33 deg |
| 32" 16:9 (70.8 cm) | 61 deg | 54 deg | 48 deg | 39 deg |
| 34" 21:9 (79.4 cm) | 67 deg | 59 deg | 53 deg | 43 deg |
| 42" 16:9 TV (93 cm) | 76 deg | 67 deg | 60 deg | 50 deg |
| 49" 32:9 (119.6 cm, flat equivalent) | 90 deg | 81 deg | 74 deg | 62 deg |
| 55" TV at 2.5 m (121.8 cm) | 27 deg at 250 cm | | | |

- Takeaway: a 55" TV across the room shows about half the correct FOV of a 27" monitor at arm's length.
- If the mathematically correct FOV feels claustrophobic on a single screen, widening it 10 to 20% is a common compromise (community practice, not sourced).

### Curved vs flat

- Curve helps when you sit centred and close: RTINGS says a curved screen is beneficial for atmospheric gaming like sim racing, and only worth it if you sit directly in front ([RTINGS curved vs flat](https://www.rtings.com/monitor/learn/curved-vs-flat)).
- Single ultrawide/super-ultrawide: curved is preferred (1800R typical; 1000R is aggressive, 800R on the LG 45").
- Triples: flat is simpler to align and match; curved works only with identical panels and correct projection. Avoid 1000R panels for triples, stick to 1500R or flatter ([Coach Dave](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/best-sim-racing-monitors/), [SimXPro](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/curved-vs-flat-monitors-for-triple-setups-what-actually-works-in-sim-racing)).

### Refresh rate, response time, VRR

- Target: 120 to 165 Hz. Coach Dave calls 144 to 165 Hz the sweet spot with diminishing returns above 240 Hz for sim racing ([Coach Dave](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/best-sim-racing-monitors/)).
- A stable frame rate beats a high peak. Cap fps a little under what your PC holds in a full-grid race start.
- VRR (G-Sync / FreeSync / HDMI 2.1 VRR): removes tearing when fps dips. Worth having. Caveat: OLED and some VA mini-LED panels show VRR flicker in dark scenes ([RTINGS LG 39GX950B](https://www.rtings.com/monitor/reviews/lg/39gx950b-b), [RTINGS AOC vs Gigabyte](https://www.rtings.com/monitor/tools/compare/gigabyte-m27q-x-vs-aoc-q27g3xmn/31657/41611)).
- Response time: marketing "1 ms" numbers are not comparable. What matters is visible smear. OLED is effectively instant (0.03 ms claims, [MSI](https://us.msi.com/blog/best-monitor-sim-racing)); budget VA panels can smear in dark scenes ([MonitorNerds](https://monitornerds.com/best-budget-gaming-monitors-under-300/)).
- Consoles: PS5 and Xbox output up to 4K 120 Hz with VRR; game support varies (shaky per title).

### OLED vs IPS vs VA

| Panel | Strengths for sim racing | Weaknesses | Typical price tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPS | Good motion, wide viewing angles (useful for the side screens of triples), consistent colour | Greyish blacks, glow in night races | Cheapest to mid |
| VA | 3x deeper blacks than IPS, most curved ultrawides are VA, cheap | Dark smear on budget models, narrow viewing angles (colour shift on angled triple side screens) | Cheapest to mid |
| VA + mini-LED | Strong HDR and contrast for the money (AOC Q27G3XMN: 336 zones) | Blooming, VRR flicker on some | Budget to mid |
| OLED / QD-OLED | Instant response, perfect blacks (night racing, VR-like contrast), now from about $300 | Burn-in risk from static HUD elements and dashboards over years (general knowledge, not sourced this session), VRR flicker, lower full-screen brightness, text fringing | Mid to high |

- Burn-in mitigation: hide static overlays or move them occasionally, use the panel's pixel refresh, check the warranty covers burn-in (many 2 to 3 year monitor warranties do; shaky, verify per model).

## 4. Triple screen specifics

### Hardware

- Three identical monitors (same model, same revision). Mixing panels makes colour and geometry matching very hard ([SimXPro](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/curved-vs-flat-monitors-for-triple-setups-what-actually-works-in-sim-racing)).
- 27" is the balanced size (about 1.6 to 1.8 m total width); 32" feels more like a cockpit but needs a bigger stand and more distance ([Coach Dave](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/best-sim-racing-monitors/)).
- Thin bezels and a VESA mount (75/100) are mandatory. Check the monitor has VESA holes before buying; some budget curved models do not (shaky per model).
- IPS preferred for the side screens (viewing angle); VA works if side screens are angled to face you.
- All three cables go into the graphics card, never the motherboard ([simracingcockpit LMU guide](https://simracingcockpit.gg/lmu-triple-screen-setup/)). Check your GPU has 3 usable outputs at your refresh rate (most have 3x DisplayPort + 1x HDMI).

### Angles and measurement

- Side screens typically 45 to 60 deg inward; angle them so each side screen faces your eyes ([SimXPro](https://simxpro.com/blogs/guides/curved-vs-flat-monitors-for-triple-setups-what-actually-works-in-sim-racing)).
- You need five numbers for the sim: screen width, screen height (or diagonal), eye distance to centre screen, side angle, bezel gap ([simracingcockpit LMU guide](https://simracingcockpit.gg/lmu-triple-screen-setup/)).
- Set physical positions first, then enter numbers. Do not tune FOV "by feel".

### Bezel kits

- ASUS ROG Bezel-Free Kit: clip-on lenses that optically hide bezels. Designed for flat monitors 27" and under at a 120 to 130 deg angle between screens (about 50 to 60 deg inward), lenses pass about 90% of light so the seam is slightly dimmer ([BoxThisLap](https://boxthislap.org/asus-rog-bezel-free-kit-for-simracing/)). Current US price not verified.
- Most people skip the kit: thin-bezel monitors plus the sim's bezel correction is good enough. Your brain ignores the bars the way it ignores A-pillars (community consensus, not sourced).

### Comfort and motion sickness

- Motion sickness in sim VR mostly comes from low or unstable frame rate and from the cockpit camera shaking relative to the world.
- iRacing's own advice: aim for 90 Hz, start with 5 to 10 minute sessions, point a small fan at your face, start in closed-cockpit cars (MX-5, Street Stock) rather than open-wheelers ([iRacing support](https://support.iracing.com/support/solutions/articles/31000173566-what-you-need-to-play-iracing-in-vr)).
- Lock the horizon / reduce head movement: AC and ACC "Lock to Horizon", AMS2 "Camera World Movement 100%", rF2 "Camera Stabilization High" ([Overtake thread](https://www.overtake.gg/threads/vr-sickness.171975/)).
- Stop at the first sign of nausea, do not push through; most people adapt over 1 to 2 weeks of short sessions ([GTPlanet](https://www.gtplanet.net/7-easy-ways-to-avoid-motion-sickness-in-vr/)).
- Spins and crashes are the worst trigger: close your eyes or look at the dash.
- Heat and sweat: a fan is close to mandatory. Weight matters over long stints: Quest 3 about 515 g, Crystal Light 815 g, Beyond 2 107 g (sources above).
- Glasses: prescription lens inserts are the comfortable fix (not priced this session).
- You cannot see your hands: keep controls on the wheel, learn button positions by feel, or use passthrough (Quest 3) where the software supports it.

- Rule of thumb by headset (my synthesis, medium confidence):
  - Quest 3S / Quest 3 / PS VR2: RTX 5060 Ti 16 GB or RX 9060 XT 16 GB minimum, RTX 5070 / RX 9070 comfortable.

### Wheel compatibility rules

- Console wheels need a licensed security chip. A PC wheel does not "just work" on a console.
- **PlayStation: the chip is in the wheel base.** A PC-only base can never become PlayStation compatible by changing the rim ([Fanatec help](https://help.fanatec.com/hc/en-us/articles/30312872074513-Which-Wheel-Bases-can-be-used-on-PS5-or-PS4)).
- **Xbox (Fanatec ecosystem): the chip is in the steering wheel rim.** An Xbox-licensed rim makes the attached Fanatec base and pedals Xbox compatible ([Fanatec help](https://help.fanatec.com/hc/en-us/articles/30485971355793-Playstation-and-Xbox-compatible-sim-setup)).
- Other brands sell separate PlayStation and Xbox SKUs of the same base; buy the one matching your console. Every console wheel also works on PC.

### Limitations vs PC

- One display only: no triples, no 21:9/32:9 rendering (shaky on aspect ratio per title).
- VR only via PS VR2, and effectively only Gran Turismo 7 among sims.
- No mods (Assetto Corsa's main appeal), no Content Manager.
- No iRacing, AMS2, rFactor 2, BeamNG, LMU (yet), AC EVO (yet).
- No SimHub, Crew Chief, Racelab, telemetry dashboards via PC software; bass shakers need an audio-extractor workaround ([ButtKicker](https://www.thebuttkicker.com/products/buttkicker-gamer-plus)). Some games (F1, GT7, Forza) broadcast UDP telemetry that a PC or phone app on the same network can read (medium confidence, not verified this session).
- No mixing of brands for pedals/shifters; limited button boxes.
- Online play needs PS Plus / Game Pass Core for most titles (not priced this session).
- Upsides: zero setup, consistent performance, couch-friendly, GT7 and Forza are polished and forgiving, good matchmaking in GT7 Sport mode.

### GT7 and Forza as on-ramps

- GT7 (PS5): licence tests teach braking and lines, Sport mode gives clean scheduled online races, wide wheel support, optional PS VR2. Best "I own a PS5 already" starting point.
- Forza Motorsport (Xbox/PC): approachable physics, but development has ended; Forza Horizon 6 is the active Forza title and is arcade.
- If you own a console: buy a console-compatible wheel that also works on PC (all do), start in GT7/Forza/ACC, move to PC later without replacing the wheel.

## 8. Audio

### Why it matters

- Tyre scrub and squeal tell you grip is going before the wheel does: understeer, oversteer, lockups ([Fanatec](https://www.fanatec.com/explorer/games/gaming-tips/best-audio-setups-for-sim-racing-headphones-vs-speakers/)).
- Engine note is your shift light and wheelspin detector, especially with a narrow FOV where you cannot see the dash.
- Positional audio places cars alongside when you cannot see them.
- Kerb strikes, bottoming and gravel are heard as much as felt on entry-level wheels.
- Tune the mix: lower engine/ambient/wind, raise tyres and opponent cars; clean beats loud ([Fanatec](https://www.fanatec.com/explorer/games/gaming-tips/best-audio-setups-for-sim-racing-headphones-vs-speakers/)).

### Wheel-mounted vs chassis-mounted

- Wheel-mounted (buttons/encoders/screen on the rim): reachable without letting go, turns with the wheel; best for race-critical controls (brake bias, TC, radio, pit limiter). Cost: you pay per rim, and it ties you to one ecosystem.
- Chassis-mounted (box or DDU fixed to the rig): works with any wheel and any brand, cheap, good for set-and-forget controls (ignition, lights, wipers, pit menu, camera). You take a hand off the wheel.
- Dash display on the base vs in the rim: base-mounted DDU stays level and readable; in-rim screens rotate with the wheel (fine for formula/GT rims with under 360 deg use).
- Typical sensible path: wheel buttons, then phone dash (free), then one chassis button box ($80 to $200), then a DDU if you want it.

## 13. Common beginner mistakes and myths

- **"Bigger screen = more immersive."** Distance matters as much as size. A 27" at 60 cm fills more of your vision than a 55" TV at 2.5 m (section 2 table).
- **Leaving the default FOV.** Defaults are far too wide; you will misjudge every braking point. Calculate it once.
- **"Triples need a monster PC."** Triple 1080p is about 3x 1080p pixels, similar to a single 4K. A mid GPU handles it in iRacing/AC. Triple 1440p is what needs the expensive card.
- **Buying triples for F1 25 / Forza / EA WRC.** Those only stretch one image across three screens (section 4).
- **Buying three different monitors, or 1000R curved ones, for triples.** Match the model; keep to flat or 1500R+.
- **Using desk monitor arms for triples.** They sag. Budget a real stand.
- **Plugging a monitor into the motherboard instead of the GPU.**
- **"240 Hz or it is pointless."** 120 to 165 Hz stable is the sweet spot for sims; a locked 90 beats a wobbly 160.
- **Skimping on CPU.** Sims with big grids are CPU-limited; a mid GPU with an X3D CPU often races smoother than the reverse.
- **Buying an 8 GB GPU for triples or VR in 2026.** Aim for 12 to 16 GB.
- **Trusting old price guides.** 2026 prices for GPUs, RAM, consoles and Quest headsets are well above 2024 to 2025 numbers.
- **"Build it yourself, it is always cheaper."** Not in 2026 under about $2,000; compare prebuilts.
- **Waiting for the next GPU generation.** RTX 60 is rumoured for H2 2027 or later and the 50 Super refresh is postponed.
- **"VR is cheap because the headset is $350."** The GPU to drive it well is the real cost.
- **Pushing through VR nausea.** Makes it worse and can put you off for good. Short sessions, fan, stable fps, locked horizon.
- **Buying a high-end VR headset first.** Try a Quest (or a friend's) before spending $900+.
- **"A PC wheel will work on my PlayStation with an adapter."** Console support is decided by the licensed chip (base for PlayStation). Check the exact SKU.
- **"iRacing is on console now."** iRacing Arcade and NASCAR 25 are different games. The iRacing service is Windows only.
- **"F1 26 is the new game to buy."** There is no F1 26; it is F1 25 plus a paid 2026 Season Pack.
- **"OLED will burn in within months."** Risk is real with static HUDs over long periods but manageable; equally, do not ignore it: vary overlays and use panel care features. (Not independently sourced this session.)
- **Bluetooth headphones for racing.** Latency. Use wired or a 2.4 GHz dongle.
- **Ignoring audio.** Running TV speakers with music on hides tyre and engine cues that make you faster.
- **Buying a button box and DDU on day one.** Wheel buttons + keyboard + free phone dash first.
- **Paying for software before trying the free tier.** Crew Chief is free; SimHub, Racelab, iOverlay, Garage 61 all have usable free tiers.
- **Downloading "SimHub license" files from random sites.** Malware risk.
- **Plugging everything into one unpowered USB hub.** Causes random disconnects mid-race.
- **Mounting the screen on a desk that the wheel shakes.** Force feedback will wobble the image; isolate the stand or stiffen the mount.
